PandaPick

July 2026 Farhaan Beeharry
web app multi-tenant saas react typescript supabase postgresql team games
PandaPick is a friendly office prediction game — the risk-free way to turn watercooler chatter into a company-wide sport. An admin creates a workspace, invites the team by e-mail or a share link, and everyone makes light-hearted picks on challenges. Lock one in before the deadline, earn +1 point when you are right, and climb a live leaderboard that crowns the team's reigning champion. 🐼👑

Not a gambling product — no real money, no odds, no payouts. Just team morale.

Underneath the panda it is a real multi-tenant SaaS: React + TypeScript on Supabase, with every business rule enforced in Row Level Security, database triggers and fourteen Deno edge functions rather than in the interface that happens to be showing.

1 · Multi-workspace, and the limit is on creating
One account belongs to as many teams as invite it, and you switch between them in a click. The plan cap applies only to the workspaces you create — an invited membership is always free and unlimited, because charging somebody for being invited to their own colleague's team is the fastest way to make a morale product feel like a tax.

The limits that do apply — weekly picks, member count — are taken from the workspace owner's account plan, resolved by one stable function rather than stored on the workspace row, so an owner upgrading lifts their team immediately and a workspace cannot drift out of step with whoever is actually paying for it. Both caps are database triggers.

2 · The picks, and the rules that are not in the UI
  • A challenge takes as many outcomes as you like, with a deadline; picks close automatically when it passes
  • One vote per person per challenge — a unique constraint, not a disabled button
  • A creator cannot vote on their own challenge. They earn no points on it, so being able to cast a pick would be either meaningless or an exploit depending on how the scoring changed next. It is enforced in the votes insert policy, not just hidden
  • A correct pick is worth +1, awarded only at resolution. Re-resolving a challenge reverses the points it previously awarded before awarding the new ones, so an admin correcting a mistake does not inflate the leaderboard
  • Admins resolve anything; creators resolve their own only if the workspace setting allows it
  • A removed or deactivated member cannot vote — membership checks test for status = 'active', so revoking access is immediate rather than cosmetic
  • A workspace always keeps at least one active admin

Alongside the picks are two daily mini-games, both scored into the same leaderboard.

3 · Mathdown — a 1v1 maths showdown
Challenge an online teammate and you both receive the same ten questions of ramping difficulty, one at a time, in sync. The first correct answer claims that question's point and advances both players. Most points wins; ties break on the lower total solve time, measured by the server.

The answers live in a solutions table with RLS enabled and zero policies — the strongest statement Postgres can make, because there is no policy for a client to satisfy. They are checked only inside a security-definer function, so they never reach a browser at all, and the timing is measured server-side rather than reported by the player.

Two fairness fixes are worth naming:
  • A synchronised start. Starting both first-question clocks at "accept" quietly disadvantaged the challenger, who enters play a beat later. Now each player runs a countdown and signals ready, and the server starts both clocks at the same instant
  • Time weighs against correctness in the daily puzzle. A flat point per correct answer meant a higher correct count always beat a lower one and speed barely mattered — a slow perfect run beat a fast near-perfect one every time. Each correct answer now decays with the time taken on that question, from full value when answered instantly down to a floor of a fifth at twenty-five seconds. A correct answer always outscores a wrong one, but a clearly faster near-perfect run can now overtake a slow perfect one

The daily variant gives the whole workspace one shared ten-question puzzle per day; at the deadline the best score takes +2 and the runner-up +1. Recomputing the formula only rewrites puzzles still open — revealed ones keep the scores their podium was already awarded on.

4 · Pinpoint — a daily treasure hunt with a lion in it
Each workspace gets one board per day with a treasure hidden at a random point. Members drop a flag, drag it around live while they argue about it, then lock it in. At the workspace's deadline the board closes, the treasure is revealed, and the closest locked flags score.

The treasure's real coordinates sit in the same kind of unreadable table as Mathdown's answers — RLS on, no policies — and are only written into the publicly visible game row at reveal time. Until then there is nothing on the wire to inspect.

Around that sits a surprising amount of design:
  • A flag publishes its position and the panda's facing while it is being dragged, so the board is genuinely live — but only a locked flag scores
  • A lion prowls the board on a path generated at board creation: he walks for a few minutes at a time before he is allowed to nap, naps are short, and he is never asleep near the deadline — a predator who happens to be dozing at the reveal is not a hazard, he is a decoration
  • The treasure is placed away from wherever the lion will be standing at the deadline, so the game cannot ask you to plant your flag on top of him
  • Players choose the animal that represents them, stored on the profile so the whole workspace sees your choice. Skins are unlocked by slots rather than spending: every ten earned points grants one more animal you may own, so unlocking never lowers your leaderboard position. An earlier version charged points and made cosmetics compete with standing
  • A per-workspace weekend pause: with it off, no board is created at all and the app says "come back Monday", rather than opening an empty board nobody will play

5 · The platform underneath the games
  • Its own 6-digit OTP. Sign-up sends a code from an edge function, and only after verification is the workspace created through a single RPC — so a half-verified account never leaves an orphaned workspace behind
  • Invitation-only membership, with invitations that can be resent, revoked, accepted from the e-mail link, or accepted from inside the app without the raw token
  • Ownership transfer, so a workspace outlives the person who happened to create it
  • Notifications on three channels from one row. An insert into notifications triggers both the e-mail function and the Web Push function, so every current and future place that creates a notification gets push for free — a browser refreshing its keys upserts on its unique endpoint rather than accumulating duplicate subscriptions
  • @-mentions in comments, delivered by the same webhook pattern
  • Custom colour-coded categories, private, public and anonymous challenges, per-company branding, a searchable admin audit trail, seven responsive HTML e-mail templates, dark mode and EN/FR
  • Pre-auth flows — the OTP and invitation inspection — run with JWT verification off by declaration in the function config, and every other function requires a real caller

A note on how it was built
The migration list reads like a changelog because it is one: the lion started as a leopard, cost him a rename; the unlock economy was tuned three times before it landed on slots; the Mathdown scoring was rewritten once the first version's flaw showed up in real standings. Each of those is a small file with a comment at the top saying what was wrong with the previous behaviour. That is what a game running against real colleagues actually looks like — the rules are not right the first time, and the record of why they changed is worth as much as the rules.
